Most of my work happens in a browser, and most of my thinking gets lost on the way to wherever I meant to write it down.

You know the sequence. You’re reading something, an idea shows up, and you switch to your notes app. Thirty seconds later you’re answering a message that has nothing to do with the idea. The note either never gets written or gets written badly. This tiny leak happens dozens of times a week, and no productivity system I’ve tried fixes it, because the problem isn’t discipline. It’s distance.

Extensions shrink that distance, which is why a small set of them has become standard equipment for people who work in Chrome. Here are the ones I keep seeing on other people’s toolbars.

### The extensions people actually keep

**Todoist** puts your task list one click away. You can add a task from any page, and it can attach the page you were reading. If your life runs on due dates and recurring chores, it’s hard to beat.

**Grammarly** checks your writing wherever you type: email, forms, comment boxes. It won’t make you a better thinker, but it will stop you from sending “teh” to a client.

**OneTab** takes the forty tabs you’re afraid to close and turns them into a single list. Your laptop fan calms down, and you can restore the pile when you’re ready.

**Momentum** replaces the new tab page with a calm dashboard: a daily focus, a clock, a small to-do list. It’s more of a mood setter than a tool, and plenty of people swear by it.

**Notion Web Clipper** and **Evernote Web Clipper** save articles and pages into the apps they belong to. If you already live in Notion or Evernote, they’re the obvious way to feed those libraries.

**Google Keep** has a Chrome extension that saves a page or a quick note to your Keep account, and it syncs to your phone.

All of these are good. I use some of them. But look at what the last three have in common: they send your material somewhere else. The clipper hands the page to a separate app. Keep hands it to a separate service. You still need an account, a destination, and later a trip back to find the thing.

### The gap

Clippers are great at saving. They’re less good at thinking.

Thinking needs a blank page that opens instantly, stays out of the way, and sits next to whatever you’re reading. A full note-taking app in its own window asks you to leave the page. A clipper asks you to file something before you’ve decided what it means. Neither helps when you just want to type three lines about the article in front of you.

This is where a notes extension has a real structural advantage. It lives in the same place as the thing you’re reading. One keystroke or one click opens it. The browser’s side panel can hold it open next to the page, so you read on the left and write on the right without switching windows. And since it’s an extension rather than a website, it can offer things a web app can’t: a right-click menu that saves a highlighted passage as a quote with its source link, or a button that clips the current page into your notes.

None of that is magic. It’s just fewer steps. But fewer steps is the entire game, because the idea you didn’t write down is the one you’ve already lost.

### What we built

Fornya Notes is our attempt at that missing piece: a full notes workspace that lives inside Chrome. We tried to make it feel like the notes app you’d build if you started from the browser instead of the desktop.

It has a block editor, so you type a slash on an empty line and pick a heading, a to-do, a list, a quote, a callout, or a code block. Markdown shortcuts work too, so “## “ becomes a heading and “\[\] “ becomes a checkbox. Select any text and a small bar appears for bold, italic, links, and inline code. Pages can nest inside other pages, and you can star the ones you use most.

Tasks have their own view with due dates, priorities, and filters for open, today, upcoming, and done. Overdue tasks turn into white-on-black chips, which is the closest we could get to red alert lights in a theme that only has black and white. That constraint was deliberate. We wanted something quiet enough to leave open all day.

For capture, the toolbar popup has a quick-note box and a “Clip page” button. Both drop into an Inbox page you can sort out later. Ctrl/Cmd + K opens a search that looks through page titles and the text inside them. There’s also a note for today’s date that you can open from the sidebar, which turns out to be how a lot of people start using it.

Everything is stored locally in your browser. There’s no account to create and no server on our end, so nothing you write leaves your machine. You can export any page as Markdown and back up the whole workspace as a JSON file.

### Where it isn’t the right choice

I’d rather say this up front than have you find out later.

Fornya Notes doesn’t sync between devices yet, so your notes live in one browser. There’s no phone app. There’s no real-time collaboration, so it won’t replace a shared team wiki. It works in Chrome and other Chromium browsers like Edge and Brave, but not Firefox or Safari. And the editor doesn’t have tables or image embeds in this first version.

If you run a team’s documentation or need your notes on your phone all day, Notion or Google Keep is the better home, and a clipper is a fine way to feed it. Fornya is for the other 90 percent of note-taking: the quick capture, the half-formed idea, the running list for the thing you’re working on right now.

### A simple way to try it

Install it, pin it to your toolbar, and open the side panel next to the next article you read. Write down two things you’d normally keep in your head. Then press Ctrl/Cmd + K and search for one of them.

If that feels faster than what you do today, keep it. If it doesn’t, you’ve lost about two minutes, and you can export everything with one click on the way out.
